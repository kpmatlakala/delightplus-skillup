## Activity 1 – What are the parameters of a problem?

**Answer:**  
Parameters are the fixed limits that control the way in which you need to solve a problem. They define the boundaries, constraints, and conditions within which a solution must be found.

**Example from the learner guide:**  
When moving computers and storing them temporarily, the parameters include:
- The company’s policies and procedures
- The availability of space
- The time constraints (delivery date vs moving date)
- Safety regulations
- Budget limitations

**Key point:** Parameters help you understand what is possible and what is not allowed when solving the problem. Before solving any problem, you must identify its parameters.

---

## Activity 2 – Once you have collected information about a problem, what is the next step in the process of solving the problem?

**Answer:**  
The next step is to **ensure that the information meets the problem requirements**. This means checking whether the information you have gathered is useful and relevant to solving the problem.

**Explanation:**
- Not all collected information will help you solve the problem.
- You must evaluate whether the information:
  - Is accurate and complete
  - Directly relates to the problem type, parameters, and possible causes
  - Can lead to a practical solution

**Example from the learner guide:**  
In the office moving example, collecting information about storage space and moving dates is helpful. However, collecting information about what software new employees will need does not help solve the storage problem. That information does NOT meet the problem requirements.

Only after you have confirmed that the information is sufficient and relevant do you proceed to determine a course of action.

---

## Activity 3 – Define the process of determining a course of action

**Answer:**  
Determining a course of action means forming ideas about what you will do to solve the problem – **without actually taking action yet**. It is the planning stage where you consider possible solutions.

**Key characteristics:**
- You brainstorm different ways to solve the problem.
- You do NOT implement anything at this stage.
- You evaluate the feasibility, risks, and benefits of each possible action.

**Example from the learner guide:**  
In the office moving problem, you consider storing the new computers in the boardroom. You cannot actually put them there yet because the boardroom table is still in place, but you identify this as a possible course of action.

**Step in the conceptual model:**  
This is Step 2 (after defining the problem and collecting information, before evaluating solutions).

## Activity 4 – Define any three common computer problems

**Answer (any three of the following):**

1. **Insufficient Memory** – The computer does not have enough RAM (Random Access Memory) to run programs efficiently. Symptoms include slowdowns, crashes, and inability to open multiple applications at once.

2. **Adware** – Unwanted software that displays pop‑up advertisements and banners. It is annoying but generally easy to remove with anti‑malware tools.

3. **Slowdown** – The computer operates much slower than usual. Common causes include a fragmented hard drive, too many background processes, or an overtaxed CPU.

4. **Weird Noises** – Unusual clicks, grinding, or whirring sounds from the computer. This often signals a mechanical failure (e.g., failing hard drive or fan) or a loose part inside the case.

5. **Failing Power Supply** – The computer shuts down suddenly or has difficulty starting up. Without sufficient stable power, the system cannot operate properly.

6. **Overheating** – The computer becomes very hot and may shut down to prevent damage. Causes include dust‑clogged fans, poor ventilation, or a failing cooling system. In extreme cases, overheating can be a fire hazard.

## Activity 6 – Define the following user support problems: Hardware failure; Software issue and User-created problem

**Answer:**

| Problem Type | Definition |
|--------------|------------|
| **Hardware failure** | A physical component of the computer (e.g., motherboard, hard drive, power supply, cable) has stopped working correctly or is completely broken. It can also include simple issues like an unplugged cable or a router/modem that needs restarting. |
| **Software issue** | A problem related to the operating system, an application, a Windows update, or a software bug. The hardware is working correctly, but the software behaves unexpectedly or crashes. |
| **User-created problem** | A problem caused unintentionally by the user, such as disabling a feature, changing a setting, deleting a necessary file, or installing conflicting software. The user may not realise they caused the issue. |

**Additional categories from the learner guide (for context):**  
- **Training or documentation** – No real technical problem; the user lacks knowledge or the documentation is outdated.  
- **Outside vendor issue** – A third‑party product (custom software or specialised hardware) is causing the problem; support obligation often rests with the vendor.

## Activity 7 – Explain how to review a solution

**Answer:**  
Reviewing a solution means **evaluating or analysing it** after implementation to see whether it works as expected and whether any changes are needed.

**Steps to review a solution (from the learner guide example of tidying bedroom cupboards):**

1. **Compare the outcome to the original problem definition** – What was the problem? (e.g., not finding clothes quickly in the morning).

2. **Ask specific evaluation questions:**  
   - Does the solution save me time?  
   - Can I find what I need more easily now?  
   - Does the solution work as well as I expected?

3. **Check if the solution achieved its intended goals** – If yes, the review is positive. If not, the solution needs modification.

**Key principle:** Reviewing is not a one‑time event. It should be done periodically, especially after trailing (following up) the solution.

## Activity 8 – Why is it important to consult stakeholders?

**Answer:**  
Consulting stakeholders (everyone who is involved in or affected by the problem and its solution) is important because:

1. **Coordination** – Different people may have different tasks that need to be aligned. One person finishing early does not mean the whole project is done; others may need more time.

2. **Buy‑in and acceptance** – People are more likely to support a solution if they were consulted during its development.

3. **Identifying hidden constraints** – Stakeholders may know about limitations or requirements that the problem solver does not.

4. **Avoiding negative impacts** – A solution that works for one person might create new problems for another stakeholder. Consulting prevents unintended consequences.

5. **Improving the solution** – Different perspectives can lead to a more effective, durable solution.

**Example from the learner guide:** In a group project, one student finishes early but the rest of the group still needs time. The early finisher must consult the group to coordinate the final submission.

## Activity 9 – Why is it useful to standardise a solution?

**Answer:**  
Standardising a solution means using the same proven method to solve all problems of the same type. It is useful because:

1. **Consistency** – Everyone follows the same procedure, reducing errors and confusion.

2. **Efficiency** – You do not need to reinvent the solution each time; time is saved.

3. **Quality assurance** – Standardised solutions have already been tested and proven to work.

4. **Training** – New staff can be trained quickly on a standard procedure.

5. **Reliability** – Customers and users know what to expect.

**Example from the learner guide:**  
- **Supermarket egg boxes** – Eggs break easily, so supermarkets standardised packaging (protective boxes). This solution works best, so it is used for all eggs.  
- **School uniforms** – A standardised way of dressing that is practical and fair.

**Key quote from the learner guide:**  
> “When you standardise a solution to a problem, you are saying: This way of solving a problem works the best. So I am going to use it to solve all problems of this type.”

## Activity 10 (5 marks) – Outline the general factors that are contained in a record

**Answer:**  
When closing a resolved computer problem, the record must contain the following factors:

1. What the original problem was (clear description)  
2. An explanation of the symptoms (error messages, behaviour)  
3. When the user first reported the problem (date and time)  
4. An outline of all troubleshooting steps taken  
5. Whether the problem was fixed or forwarded (to whom, when)  
6. When the computer was returned to the user (date and time)  
7. What was done to fix it (repairs, replacements, configuration changes)  

*(1 mark for each correct factor, up to 5 marks)*

## Activity 11 (5 marks) – List the techniques of solving problems

**Answer (three main techniques from the learner guide):**

1. **Comparing the problem to information about previous problems** – Use past solutions to solve similar issues quickly.  
2. **Troubleshooting (elimination)** – Systematically eliminate all possible causes until only the real cause remains.  
3. **Seeking expert help** – Consult external sources (internet, manufacturers, computer shops).  

*(2 marks for the first two techniques, 1 mark for the third – total 5 marks)*

**Additional techniques (for extra context – not required for marks):**  
Reboot, replicate, retrace steps, check Device Manager, review error logs, isolate the problem, seek obvious solutions.

## Activity 12 (8 marks) – Discuss unresolved computer problems and support agreements

**Answer:**

When a problem cannot be resolved within the terms of the support agreement (SLA), the following must be considered:

1. **Reporting the extension** – Inform the user as soon as possible that the fix will take longer than agreed. Explain why and what you are doing. *(2 marks)*

2. **Find a fix before calling the user** – First identify who can resolve the issue (e.g., a specialist vendor). Contact that person to arrange a solution, then give the user a clear plan – not just bad news. *(2 marks)*

3. **Provide a temporary computer** – If the user’s computer will be unavailable beyond the SLA, offer a replacement or loan device to minimise disruption. *(1 mark)*

4. **Advise third parties** – Keep other departments or vendors informed of progress according to the support agreement terms. *(1 mark)*

5. **Record additional information** – Document everything tried, why internal resolution failed, and the escalation steps taken. *(1 mark)*

6. **Car workshop analogy (good practice)** – The manager phones to say they cannot fix a damaged seat but are looking for another place. The customer is unhappy but satisfied that the manager is doing everything possible. *(1 mark)*

**Conclusion:** How you report unresolved problems is as important as the technical solution. Honesty, proactive communication, and offering alternatives maintain user trust even when the news is unfavourable.
