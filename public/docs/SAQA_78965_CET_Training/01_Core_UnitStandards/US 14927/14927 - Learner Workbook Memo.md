## Activity 1 (5 marks) – Identify causes of problems in the workplace

*Any 5 of the following common causes:*

1. **Poor communication** – Instructions, expectations, or feedback are not clearly shared, leading to misunderstandings and errors.  
2. **Lack of resources** – Insufficient time, budget, equipment, staff, or materials to complete tasks properly.  
3. **Unclear roles and responsibilities** – Employees do not know exactly what they are supposed to do, causing duplication or omission of work.  
4. **Inadequate training** – Workers lack the necessary skills or knowledge to perform their duties correctly.  
5. **Poor processes or procedures** – Outdated, overly complex, or non‑existent workflows create bottlenecks and mistakes.  
6. **Low employee morale** – Lack of motivation, recognition, or fair treatment leads to disengagement and reduced productivity.  
7. **Equipment or technology failure** – Hardware, software, or network breakdowns disrupt operations.  
8. **External factors** – Supplier delays, market changes, legal regulations, or economic conditions beyond the organisation’s control.

## Activity 2 (10 marks) – Draw a Map showing the problem solving process

*Since a drawing cannot be rendered in text, the following is a description of the standard problem‑solving process map. Learners should produce a flowchart with these steps.*

**Generic problem‑solving process (e.g., IDEAL model or PDCA):**

1. **Identify the problem** – Recognise that a problem exists.  
2. **Define the problem** – Clarify the problem type, parameters, and possible causes.  
3. **Collect information** – Gather relevant facts, data, and stakeholder input.  
4. **Analyse the problem** – Break it into components; identify root causes.  
5. **Generate possible solutions** – Brainstorm multiple alternatives.  
6. **Evaluate alternatives** – Weigh pros and cons against established criteria.  
7. **Select the best solution** – Choose the most feasible, effective, and acceptable option.  
8. **Plan implementation** – Develop action steps, timelines, resources, and responsibilities.  
9. **Implement the solution** – Execute the plan.  
10. **Monitor and evaluate** – Check if the solution works; make adjustments if needed.  
11. **Standardise** – If successful, document and adopt as a standard practice.

**Flowchart shape key (for drawing):**  
- Oval = Start / End  
- Rectangle = Process step  
- Diamond = Decision (e.g., “Is solution effective?”)  
- Arrow = Direction of flow

## Activity 3 (5 marks) – List factors that can contribute to solving the problem

*Factors that help solve problems effectively:*

1. **Clear problem definition** – Understanding exactly what the problem is (type, parameters, causes).  
2. **Accurate and sufficient information** – Relevant data that helps identify root causes.  
3. **Creative thinking** – Ability to generate multiple innovative solutions.  
4. **Team collaboration** – Diverse perspectives and shared knowledge.  
5. **Time and resources** – Adequate budget, tools, and deadlines.  
6. **Strong communication** – Open sharing of ideas and feedback.  
7. **Decision‑making criteria** – Pre‑defined standards to evaluate solutions objectively.  
8. **Leadership support** – Authority and encouragement to implement changes.  
9. **Willingness to take calculated risks** – Trying new approaches without fear of failure.  
10. **Monitoring and feedback mechanisms** – Ability to track results and adjust as needed.

## Activity 4 (3 marks) – Outline the importance of evaluating your resources and strengths in solving the problem

Evaluating your resources and strengths before solving a problem is important because:

1. **Feasibility check** – It helps determine whether a solution is realistic given the available budget, people, equipment, and time.  
2. **Efficiency** – Using existing strengths (e.g., skilled staff, established tools) avoids reinventing the wheel and speeds up implementation.  
3. **Risk reduction** – Knowing your limitations prevents overcommitment and failure; you can plan to acquire missing resources or adjust the solution.  

In short, you cannot solve a problem effectively if you do not know what you have to work with. Honest self‑assessment leads to practical, achievable solutions.

## Activity 5 (7 marks) – Demonstrate understanding of how to develop/weigh alternatives

Developing and weighing alternatives (also called “evaluating options”) is a critical step in problem solving. The process involves:

### 1. Generate multiple alternatives
- **Brainstorming** – List as many possible solutions as possible without judging them initially.  
- **Research** – Look for best practices, case studies, or expert advice.  
- **Combine ideas** – Create hybrid solutions from different suggestions.

### 2. Establish evaluation criteria (aligned to the problem)
Typical criteria include:  
- **Effectiveness** – How well does it solve the root cause?  
- **Feasibility** – Can we do it with available resources?  
- **Cost** – Financial, time, and effort required.  
- **Time to implement** – How quickly can it be done?  
- **Risk** – What could go wrong?  
- **Acceptability** – Will stakeholders support it?  
- **Sustainability** – Will the solution last?

### 3. Weigh the alternatives (compare)
- Use a **decision matrix** – Score each option against the criteria (e.g., 1‑5 scale). Multiply by importance weight. Sum scores.  
- **Pros and cons list** – Simple but effective for less complex problems.  
- **Cost‑benefit analysis** – Quantify expected benefits minus costs.  
- **Risk assessment** – Identify high‑risk options and decide if the risk is acceptable.

### 4. Select the best alternative
- Choose the option with the highest overall score / best balance of pros and cons.  
- Consider a pilot test for uncertain solutions before full implementation.

**Example (simple decision matrix):**

| Criteria (weight) | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|------------------|----------|----------|----------|
| Effectiveness (5) | 4 (20)   | 5 (25)   | 3 (15)   |
| Cost (3)         | 2 (6)    | 3 (9)    | 5 (15)   |
| Time (2)         | 3 (6)    | 4 (8)    | 2 (4)    |
| **Total**        | **32**   | **42**   | **34**   |

Option B is best.

## Activity 6 (10 marks) – Describe how to implement the problem solving solution

Implementation is putting the chosen solution into action. The following steps ensure effective implementation:

### 1. Develop an action plan
- **Break down the solution** into specific, manageable tasks.  
- **Assign responsibilities** – Who does what?  
- **Set deadlines** – Realistic timelines for each task.  
- **Allocate resources** – Budget, equipment, people.

### 2. Communicate the plan
- Inform all stakeholders (team, management, affected users) about what will change, why, and when.  
- Address concerns and get buy‑in.

### 3. Pilot test (if possible)
- Test the solution on a small scale before full rollout.  
- Identify problems early and adjust.

### 4. Execute the plan
- Carry out the tasks according to the schedule.  
- Monitor progress closely (use checklists, status meetings).

### 5. Monitor and trail the solution (from learning outcomes)
- **Trail** = follow up after implementation. Check if the problem is truly solved.  
- **Monitor** = collect data on performance (e.g., error rates, user satisfaction).  
- If the solution is not working as expected, go back to review and modify.

### 6. Review and modify
- Evaluate the solution against the original problem definition.  
- Ask: “Does it work? Does it last?”  
- If not, make changes (modify approach, reallocate resources, or choose another alternative).

### 7. Standardise successful solutions
- Once a solution proves effective, document it as a standard procedure.  
- Train others on the new standard.  
- Update policies, manuals, or checklists.

### 8. Consult stakeholders throughout
- Keep affected parties informed and involved.  
- Seek feedback to improve the solution.

### 9. Close the problem
- Formally record what was done, results achieved, and lessons learned.  
- Celebrate success and recognise contributions.

**Example:** A company solves slow computer performance by upgrading RAM. Implementation:  
- Plan: Purchase RAM, schedule after‑hours installation, assign technician.  
- Pilot: Upgrade one department first.  
- Monitor: Measure boot time and application response.  
- Standardise: Make 8GB RAM the new minimum standard for all workstations.
